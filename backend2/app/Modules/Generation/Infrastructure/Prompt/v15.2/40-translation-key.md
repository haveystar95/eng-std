## The translation is a KEY, not a description

`translation` and `example_translation` are not prose and not dictionary entries. Each is the
QUESTION the learner is shown, and the {{target_lang}} side is the ONLY answer that will be
accepted. A learner who reads your {{source_lang}} line and writes the {{target_lang}} line back
must be marked right.

The test that decides every one of them:

> **Cover the {{target_lang}} side. Reading only your {{source_lang}} line, could someone who does
> not know the term write `text` back — that expression, not a paraphrase of its meaning?**

If the answer is no, the line is broken, however true it is. There are three ways to break it.

### 1. A DEFINITION instead of a key

The most damaging and the easiest to write by accident: explaining what the term MEANS instead of
saying it in {{source_lang}}.

- `back up` → «подниматься обратно из-за засора» — that is a description of a blocked drain. It is
  correct about the world and useless as a key: nobody reads it and writes `back up`.
  The key is «забиваться» / «идти обратно».
- `run out of` → «когда что-то закончилось и больше нет» — a definition. The key is «закончиться».

Rules that follow:

- A translation is **the shortest {{source_lang}} expression a native would use for the term** —
  normally one to three words for a word, and a mirror of the phrase for a phrase.
- **No explanatory clauses.** If your translation contains «когда…», «то есть…», «из-за…»,
  «который…», «чтобы…», or names a situation rather than the thing itself, you are defining.
- **No parentheses that explain the meaning.** A short parenthetical is allowed for ONE purpose
  only: to separate two senses that would otherwise be the same key — «заполнить (форму)». It is
  never a place to describe how the thing works.
- **A translation longer than its term is a warning sign.** Twice the length is almost always a
  definition that has not noticed itself.

If a term genuinely has no short {{source_lang}} equivalent, that term is a bad card — replace the
item rather than shipping a description as its question.

### 1a. A CALQUE instead of what a person actually says

A definition explains too much. A calque explains nothing and is still wrong: it is the
grammatically faithful {{source_lang}} rendering of the {{target_lang}} form — the shape of the
word carried across — instead of the words a living person would use.

**The translation is the form a living {{source_lang}} speaker would say out loud**, in the
situation the term belongs to. Not the form a grammar table would derive from `text`.

Where this goes wrong most reliably: **{{target_lang}} adjectives that name a STATE**. They are
built from verbs, so the faithful rendering is a participle — and the participle is not what anyone
says:

- `relieved` → «испытавший облегчение» is a participle nobody utters. The key is «с облегчением».
- `excited` → «пришедший в возбуждение» — same defect. The key is «в предвкушении» / «взволнованный».
- `annoyed` → «испытывающий раздражение». The key is «раздражённый».
- `overwhelmed` → «оказавшийся перегруженным». The key is «в растерянности» / «зашивающийся».

The test, and it is a different one from the definition test: **say your translation out loud as an
answer to «Как ты?» or as a caption to the situation.** If it sounds like a case report rather than
like a person — if nobody would ever say it in that shape — it is a calque, and the learner cannot
produce it from a prompt they would never phrase that way.

Prefer, in this order: the ordinary adjective («раздражённый»), the short prepositional phrase a
speaker actually uses («с облегчением», «в растерянности»), the plain verb form. A participial
construction is the last resort and almost always the wrong one — «испытавший», «оказавшийся»,
«пришедший в», «находящийся в» at the head of a translation are a sign that the shape of the
{{target_lang}} word was translated instead of its use.

This is not a licence to be loose: the addressee rules below still hold, and «с облегчением» is
right because it is what is SAID, not because it is shorter.

### 2. Something LOST

A translation that drops a word of `text` stops pointing at `text`. The words most often dropped are
the small ones that carry WHO is speaking and WHO is addressed, because a translation reads more
smoothly without them — and smoothness is not what this field is for.

Keep, one for one, in {{source_lang}}:

- **the addressee and the speaker** — every pronoun `text` uses for who speaks and who is addressed
  (`us`, `me`, `you`/`your`, `we`/`our`, `them`/`their`) needs its own explicit counterpart on the
  {{source_lang}} side, in whatever form {{source_lang}} marks it. The exact word depends on
  {{source_lang}}; the requirement does not;
- **every qualifier** — a possessive, an adjective, a number, a preposition that changes the meaning;
- **the grammatical person and number** the term is in.

Worked through on the item that failed, in Russian: `Tell us about a challenge you faced` came back
«Расскажите о вызове, с которым вы столкнулись». It reads well and it is wrong, because the word for
`us` is gone and `Tell me…`, `Tell us…` and `Describe…` all answer it equally. The learner needs
«Расскажите **нам** о вызове, с которым вы столкнулись» — clumsier by a word, and unambiguous. Same
for `your`: «Расскажите нам о **вашем** опыте», not «о своём опыте», which hides whose experience it
is. Whatever word the learner's language uses for `us`, `me`, `you`, `our` has to survive.

### 3. Something ADDED

The mirror, and easier to miss because the result reads better than the correct version. The
translation must not say anything the {{target_lang}} side never said.

`I get along with my team` came back «Я **хорошо** лажу со своей командой». Nothing licenses
«хорошо»: there is no `well`, no `good`, no `nicely` in the source. The learner writes the exact
sentence and is marked wrong.

So: no intensifier or evaluation the source has no word for; no person the source does not name; no
possessive or qualifier the source leaves open; no narrowing that makes the question a different one.

### Two more rules from the same principle

- **Never smuggle the answer in:** no {{target_lang}} words in the translation, no transliteration,
  no "(from the verb …)" spelling out the form. Disambiguate with the minimum that does the job.
- **No two items in one answer may share a `translation`.** Two identical questions with two
  different accepted answers is a card that cannot be passed by knowing the material.

**Accuracy beats fluency, and brevity beats both.** If the smooth translation and the exact one
differ, ship the exact one; if the exact one has grown into a sentence explaining the term, you have
written a definition — go back and name the thing.
